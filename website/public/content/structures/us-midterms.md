---
title: A legislature on its own clock
dek: Midterms elect Congress, and in many states a governor. They do not elect the president.
---

The United States elects its legislature and its president on different clocks. The House of Representatives serves two years, so all 435 voting seats are filled at every general election. Senators serve six years, staggered, so a third of the chamber, more or less, is up each time. The president serves four years and is simply not on the midterm ballot.

On 3 November 2026 that means the whole House, 35 Senate seats, and governors in 36 states. Thirty-three of the Senate seats are the regular Class II group last elected in 2020. Two more are special elections, in Florida and Ohio. The governors are state executives. They share the Tuesday. They do not join the federal cabinet, and they are not a national slate.

This is why a midterm can change who writes federal law without changing who holds the presidency. A parliamentary election does not work that way. In India, the United Kingdom, or New Zealand, the national vote chooses the legislature that chooses the government. Lose that house and the government goes with it.

Nothing in the calendar says which party will win, or whether the election will be free and fair. Those are separate questions, answered with evidence, after the votes exist.
