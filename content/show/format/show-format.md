# Show format

Democracy This Week is a weekend prime-time talk show. One desk. One hour and a half on a normal Sunday. Two hours when the ballot card is crowded. It is not a news bulletin and it is not a debate with a winner.

The audience should leave able to answer three questions:

1. What office was on the ballot, and on what day?
2. How does that government get chosen — by the public, by a chamber, or by both?
3. What date is next, and is that date confirmed or only a window?

## The desk

| Chair | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Host | Opens, keeps the clock, asks the one question of the night, closes. |
| Ballot correspondent | Dates, offices, terms, and whether the date is confirmed, tentative, preliminary, or still undated. |
| Structure editor | One lesson about how the office is filled. A comparison with one other country, not a tour of the world. |

A guest sits in only when they can explain a rule: an electoral commission, a parliamentary clerk, a correspondent who has read the law. A guest does not call a result.

On screen, the lower third is country, office, date, and confidence. It never carries a predicted winner or a rating.

The only prop is a paper calendar. If a day is not on that calendar, it is not said as fact.

## When it airs

Sunday evening.

The production weekend is Saturday–Sunday. In the rundowns that weekend is named by its Saturday.

- A vote on Saturday is live in that show only as **what the day is for**. The host does not read a count.
- A vote on Sunday is the show day. The first hour is the preview. The second half explains how a result would be produced. It does not invent one.
- A vote on Monday–Friday is covered on the Sunday after it, as structure, unless the Sunday before was already the preview. Then Sunday after is the desk: what the offices were, still without a winner.

## The 90-minute clock

| Clock | Block | Length | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Open | 4 min | One spoken paragraph. The office, the day, the stake. No adjective about fairness. |
| 0:04 | On the desk | 8 min | Every dated ballot in the next 14 days, in one sentence each. Tentative dates are said as tentative. |
| 0:12 | The ballot | 22 min | The main country. Who votes, for which office, for how long, and what happens if nobody clears the rule. |
| 0:34 | How this government works | 18 min | One mechanism. Parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, two-round, staggered chamber, collective presidency, or indirect election. |
| 0:52 | The other chair | 14 min | A second country that week, or an Indian assembly, or the United States. If the week has only one ballot, this chair is the comparison country. |
| 1:06 | What we will not say | 8 min | The host reads the bans for this episode: no winner, no score, no second round treated as certain when it is conditional. |
| 1:14 | Watch next | 8 min | The next weekend, then the next confirmed date in the month. |
| 1:22 | Close | 8 min | Restate the three questions. Point at the paper calendar. |

## The 120-minute clock

Use this when the rundown says 120. Add the blocks; do not talk faster.

| Clock | Block | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Open | 5 min |
| 0:05 | On the desk | 10 min |
| 0:15 | The ballot | 25 min |
| 0:40 | How this government works | 18 min |
| 0:58 | The other chair | 16 min |
| 1:14 | Third chair | 14 min |
| 1:28 | What we will not say | 8 min |
| 1:36 | The month ahead | 12 min |
| 1:48 | Watch next | 6 min |
| 1:54 | Close | 6 min |

The third chair is a real second or third country voting that weekend. It is not a panel of opinions.

## A quiet weekend

An empty week is a valid show. The ballot block becomes “nothing national is on the calendar these 14 days,” and the structure block carries the hour. The host says the empty week out loud so the audience does not think the desk forgot a country.

Quiet weekends rotate. Do not repeat the same lesson two Sundays running.

1. Why a parliament can dismiss a government and a president cannot.
2. Why some presidents need a majority and some need only to finish first.
3. Why a senate is elected in pieces.
4. Why a referendum does not choose a prime minister.
5. Why a date that says “by the end of the year” is not a polling day.
6. India: an assembly is a state government, not the Union government.
7. The United States: a midterm does not elect the president.

## A fifth weekend

January, May, August, and October 2026 have five Saturdays. January, May, July, and October 2027 do too.

The fifth show is one of these, and the rundown says which:

- The vote that falls on that Saturday or Sunday.
- A conditional second round, with two written opens.
- The bridge: the first preview of next month’s main ballot.

The short brief on the site uses four headings. The show speaks all four, then stays on the air.

| Site heading | Show block |
| --- | --- |
| Open | Open |

The extra blocks — the other chair, what we will not say, the month ahead — are what make it a 90-minute talk show rather than a four-minute read.

## Tape and live

The structure lesson can be taped in the week. The open, the desk, and watch next are live, because a commission can move a date between Tuesday and Sunday.

If a date moves after the rundown was locked, the host says the old date, the new date, and who announced the change. The structure lesson stays. The offices rarely change when a day moves.
