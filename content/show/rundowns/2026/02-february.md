# February 2026 — four weekends

Archive plans. No winners.

## Weekend of 7 February

- **Air:** Sunday 8 February, 120 minutes
- **Status:** archive plan
- **Main election:** Japan, House of Representatives; Portugal, presidential second round; Thailand, House and referendum

### Cold open

Three countries, one Sunday, three different acts. Japan elects the House of Representatives. The prime minister is not on the ballot. Portugal’s presidential second round is the Sunday that exists because 18 January did not produce a majority. Thailand elects a house and votes on a constitutional question. A referendum is not a prime minister.

### On the desk

- 6 February — Tokelau — parliament — one sentence
- 8 February — Japan — House of Representatives — today
- 8 February — Portugal — president, second round — today
- 8 February — Thailand — House of Representatives and constitutional referendum — today
- 11 February — Barbados — House of Assembly
- 12 February — Bangladesh — parliament and constitutional referendum

### The ballot

Japan first, because it is the clearest parliamentary lesson of the night. The lower house was dissolved. Dissolution means the four-year term was a maximum. After tonight, the Diet still has to produce a government. We do not produce it for them on this desk.

### How this government works

A second round is not a second election of a different office. Portugal is finishing the presidency. The prime minister stays a creature of the assembly. Put the Portuguese rule beside Japan’s rule and leave it there.

### The other chair

Thailand. Two pieces of paper: a house, and a yes-or-no. Read them as two results-that-we-will-not-call, not as one.

### Third chair

The coming week, briefly: Barbados on Wednesday is a parliamentary election. Bangladesh on Thursday is a parliament plus a referendum, the same shape as Thailand.

### The month ahead

Laos on 22 February, a one-party parliamentary election. Say that it is coming. Do not grade it tonight.

### What we will not say

- We will not name the next prime minister of Japan.
- We will not name the president of Portugal.
- We will not call Thailand’s referendum a change of government by itself.

### Watch next

Sunday 15 February, the desk on Barbados and Bangladesh.

## Weekend of 14 February

- **Air:** Sunday 15 February, 90 minutes
- **Status:** archive plan
- **Main election:** Barbados, 11 February; Bangladesh, 12 February

### Cold open

Barbados elected a House of Assembly on Wednesday. The prime minister comes out of that house. Bangladesh on Thursday elected a parliament and held a constitutional referendum. The president of Bangladesh is not chosen by the public. Parliament elects the president later in the year. No winners tonight.

### On the desk

- 11 February — Barbados — House of Assembly — held, no result on this desk
- 12 February — Bangladesh — parliament and referendum — held, no result on this desk
- 22 February — Laos — parliament

### The ballot

Spend the ballot block on Bangladesh’s two questions, because a viewer will otherwise merge them. The referendum answers a constitutional question. The parliament is a chamber. Only the chamber can later elect a president, and that vote is 20 August, and it is indirect.

### How this government works

Barbados is the comparison. A republic can still be parliamentary. The public elects the house. The house produces the government. There is no presidential Sunday hiding behind this one.

### The other chair

Laos on 22 February. Preview only: a parliamentary election in a one-party system is on our calendar, and a date is not a grade. Full block next Sunday.

### What we will not say

- We will not declare a prime minister of Barbados.
- We will not treat Bangladesh’s referendum and its parliament as one result.
- We will not say the Bangladeshi president was elected this week.

## Weekend of 21 February

- **Air:** Sunday 22 February, 90 minutes
- **Status:** archive plan
- **Main election:** Laos, parliament, this Sunday

### Cold open

Laos votes for a parliament today. The system is one-party. The desk lists the election because the calendar lists it. The desk does not score it, and it does not describe the day as a contest between alternative governments. Next month starts with Nepal’s House of Representatives on 5 March.

### On the desk

- 22 February — Laos — parliament — today
- 5 March — Nepal — House of Representatives
- 8 March — Colombia — House and Senate
- 8 March — Switzerland — referendums

### The ballot

Say what a one-party parliamentary election is, in plain words: the public is asked to vote, and the vote is not how a different party takes the government. Then sit with that sentence. Do not decorate it.

### How this government works

Use Nepal as the comparison, not as a moral. Nepal on 5 March is a parliamentary election in which the house, afterwards, is what a government must command. The mechanism is the thing being taught. The host does not rank the two countries.

### The other chair

Colombia on 8 March elects a congress and does not elect a president. The president is 31 May, with a possible second round on 21 June. Plant the split. It is one of the year’s best lessons and it deserves next month’s two-hour show.

### What we will not say

- We will not call a winner in Laos.
- We will not call the vote free, or not free, as a score. If a sourced sentence is in the script, it is read as a source. There is no such sentence in this plan.

### Watch next

Sunday 8 March. Nepal’s desk, Colombia’s congress, Switzerland’s referendum.

### The ballot

### How this government works

A snap election, using Denmark. The term is a maximum. A prime minister can send the country back early. The Faroe Islands have their own snap election on 26 March. Mention them as a home-rule parliament, not as Denmark’s election.

### The other chair
