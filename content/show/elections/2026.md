# Elections of 2026 — structure for the show

National and federal votes, plus referendums, as listed on the [2026 national electoral calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_national_electoral_calendar). Indian assemblies and the United States midterm detail live in [india.md](india.md) and [united-states.md](united-states.md). They are included here so the year reads in order.

A line is the structure the Sunday show explains. It is not a result.

Confidence: **confirmed** unless the line says tentative, indirect, or undated.

---

## January

### 11 January — Benin, parliament
The public elects the National Assembly. The president is not on this ballot. The presidential election is a separate confirmed day, 12 April. Term of the assembly is the legislative term; the show does not need a seat count to make the point that January and April are two offices.

Show: weekend of 10 January.

### 11 January — Myanmar, parliament, second phase
A phased parliamentary vote under the military administration. Phase three is 25 January. This is on the calendar. The desk does not grade it and does not describe it as an ordinary change of government. The public’s role is whatever the junta’s election plan says; the host says “phased parliamentary vote” and stops.

Show: weekend of 10 January.

### 15 January — Uganda, president and parliament
One day for the head of state and the legislature. That is a general election, not a midterm. The show’s lesson is the difference between this shape and a parliamentary election, where the head of government is chosen later inside the chamber.

Show: weekend of 17 January, as the desk (the vote was Thursday).

### 18 January — Portugal, president, first round
Portugal is semi-presidential. Voters elect the president. They do not elect the prime minister on this day. If no candidate wins a majority, the top two meet on 8 February. The government still has to live with the Assembly of the Republic, which is not being elected in January.

Show: weekend of 17 January. This Sunday is the first round.

### 25 January — Myanmar, parliament, third phase
The last published phase of the same parliamentary vote. Same rule as 11 January: calendar, not a grade.

Show: weekend of 24 January.

## February

### 1 February — Costa Rica, president and parliament
A general election. The president and the Legislative Assembly are chosen on the same Sunday. A presidential candidate who does not clear the legal threshold faces a runoff; the show states the threshold from the Supreme Electoral Tribunal’s own rule card that week rather than from memory if the card is in the studio. The assembly does not have a runoff.

Show: weekend of 31 January. That Sunday is election day.

### 6 February — Tokelau, parliament
A dependent territory, not a sovereign state. The show mentions it on the desk in one sentence on the weekend of 7 February and does not build the hour on it.

### 8 February — Japan, House of Representatives
The lower house. The prime minister is not on the ballot. After the election the Diet still has to choose who forms a government. This was a dissolution election: the chamber was sent to the country early. The lesson is that a parliamentary term is a maximum, not a promise that the chamber will sit out the years.

Show: weekend of 7 February. The vote is that Sunday.

### 8 February — Portugal, president, second round
Held only because the 18 January round did not produce a majority. By the time this book is used as an archive, the round’s existence is historical. The archive script still does not name the winner. It explains why a second Sunday exists.

Show: weekend of 7 February.

### 8 February — Thailand, House of Representatives and a constitutional referendum
Two questions on one day: who sits in the house, and a yes-or-no on the constitution. A referendum does not by itself name a prime minister. The house election and the referendum get two separate sentences on the desk.

Show: weekend of 7 February.

### 11 February — Barbados, House of Assembly
A parliamentary election. The prime minister comes out of the house, not from a separate presidential ballot. Barbados is a republic; the president is not this vote.

Show: weekend of 14 February.

### 12 February — Bangladesh, parliament and a constitutional referendum
Same shape as Thailand the same month: a chamber, plus a yes-or-no. The president of Bangladesh is chosen later by parliament (see the indirect list, 20 August) and is not this ballot.

Show: weekend of 14 February.

### 22 February — Laos, parliament
A one-party legislature election. It is on the calendar. The chooser on paper is the public; the competition is not the competition of a multi-party parliament. The host says that in one sentence and does not score the country.

Show: weekend of 21 February. That Sunday is election day.

## March

### 5 March — Nepal, House of Representatives
The lower house, after the dissolution that produced this date. The prime minister is accountable to that house. The show treats it as a parliamentary election: the ballot names members, the house names a government.

Show: weekend of 7 March, as the desk (Thursday’s vote).

### 8 March — Colombia, House of Representatives and Senate
Congress only. The president is not on this ballot. Colombia elects congress in March and the president in May, with a second round in June if needed. That split is the lesson: a voter can change the legislature and still be months away from changing the president.

Show: weekend of 7 March. That Sunday is election day.

### 8 March — Switzerland, referendums
A popular vote on issues, not an election of the Federal Council. The Federal Council is elected by parliament. Say “referendum” and do not say “the Swiss government is on the ballot.”

Show: weekend of 7 March.

### 15 March — Kazakhstan, constitutional referendum
A yes-or-no on the constitution. Not an election of a president or a parliament.

Show: weekend of 14 March.

### 15 March — North Korea, parliament
The Supreme People’s Assembly. The public vote on this calendar line is not a contest between parties for control of the government. The show lists the date, says the public is not choosing among competing governments, and moves on. No score.

Show: weekend of 14 March.

### 15 March — Republic of the Congo, president
A presidential election. The parliament is a different date (it appears again as undated on the 2027 calendar). The show does not invent the winning threshold.

Show: weekend of 14 March.

### 15 March — Vietnam, parliament
A single-party National Assembly election. Same handling as Laos: on the calendar, not graded, not described as a change of governing party.

Show: weekend of 14 March.

### 22 March — Slovenia, National Assembly
Parliamentary. The government comes from the assembly. The president of Slovenia is a separate office and is the undated 2027 presidential election, not this one.

Show: weekend of 21 March.

### 22–23 March — Italy, constitutional referendum
Two days, a yes-or-no on the constitution. The Chamber and the Senate are not being elected. Italy’s next parliamentary election is undated in 2027 and, if not called earlier, sits inside a legal window that ends in December 2027. That window is not this referendum.

Show: weekend of 21 March.

### 24 March — Denmark, parliament
A snap election of the Folketing, called in February for 24 March. Denmark has 179 seats: 175 from Denmark, two from Greenland, two from the Faroe Islands. The prime minister comes from that chamber. A snap election is the lesson: the four-year term is a maximum.

Show: weekend of 28 March.

### 26 March — Faroe Islands, parliament
A home-rule parliament, called as a snap election of its own, in the same week as Denmark. One sentence on the Denmark show. Not the lead.

Show: weekend of 28 March.

## April

### 4 April — Maldives, constitutional referendum
A yes-or-no, published as a question about holding presidential and parliamentary elections together. It does not itself elect either office.

Show: weekend of 4 April.

### 9 April — India: Assam, Kerala, Puducherry
See [india.md](india.md). Assemblies, not the Lok Sabha. Result not recorded.

Show: weekend of 11 April, as the desk, and previewed on 4 April.

### 10 April — Djibouti, president
Presidential. The parliament is not this ballot. The show does not invent the term or the threshold beyond “the presidency is the office.”

Show: weekend of 11 April.

### 12 April — Benin, president
The second half of Benin’s year. January was the assembly. April is the president. Two offices, two days, one country.

Show: weekend of 11 April. That Sunday is election day.

### 12 April — Hungary, parliament
The National Assembly. Mixed system: single-member seats plus a list. The prime minister comes from the assembly. The president of Hungary is elected later by parliament (indirect list, 11 August), not by this popular vote.

Show: weekend of 11 April.

### 12–13 April — Peru, president (first round), Chamber of Deputies and Senate
A general election across two days, and a congress that the calendar lists as two chambers. The presidential second round is 7 June if nobody wins the first. The show does not assume the June round until the first-round rule requires it. Archive note: by June the calendar does list 7 June as the presidential second round, so the June show treats it as the runoff that was held, still without a winner’s name.

Show: weekend of 11 April.

### 19 April — Bulgaria, parliament
A snap parliamentary election. The president of Bulgaria is a different office, on 25 October. Parliament in April, president in October.

Show: weekend of 18 April.

### 23 April — India: Tamil Nadu
One phase, 234 seats. See [india.md](india.md).

### 23 and 29 April — India: West Bengal
Two phases, 294 seats. See [india.md](india.md).

### 26 April — Central African Republic, National Assembly, second round
A second round of a legislative election. The show explains a two-round legislative district: the first round did not finish every seat. It does not invent which districts.

Show: weekend of 25 April.

### 30 April — Antigua and Barbuda, House of Representatives
Parliamentary. The prime minister comes from the house.

Show: weekend of 25 April, as watch-next, and the first May show can close the desk in one sentence if the Sunday of 3 May still has room. The vote itself is the Thursday after the 25 April weekend, so the 2 May show carries the desk.

## May

### 2 May — Niue, parliament
A territory. One sentence. Not the lead.

Show: weekend of 2 May.

### 4 May — India, counting day
The Election Commission scheduled counting of the five assemblies for 4 May 2026. The result is not in the website file. The show says “counting was scheduled” and “result not recorded.”

Show: weekend of 2 May.

### 12 May — Bahamas, House of Assembly
Parliamentary.

Show: weekend of 9 May as preview, weekend of 16 May as a one-sentence desk.

### 17 May — Cape Verde, parliament
The legislature. The president is a separate election on 15 November. Two offices, two dates, one small republic. That pairing is the lesson.

Show: weekend of 16 May.

### 24 May — Cyprus, parliament
The House of Representatives. The president of Cyprus is not on this ballot. Presidential and parliamentary elections are on different cycles.

Show: weekend of 23 May.

### 30 May — Malta, parliament
A snap parliamentary election. The prime minister comes from the house.

Show: weekend of 30 May.

### 31 May — Colombia, president, first round
A majority of the vote is required. The second round is 21 June if nobody has it. Congress was already elected on 8 March, so this Sunday cannot “flip the congress.”

Show: weekend of 30 May. That Sunday is election day.

### 31 May — Guinea, National Assembly
The calendar date. The cited notice is a postponement onto this day, so the correspondent says the assembly vote was reset to 31 May and does not describe an earlier cancelled day as if it had been held.

Show: weekend of 30 May.

## June

### 1 June — Ethiopia, House of Peoples’ Representatives
The lower house. Parliamentary in form. The show does not add a grade.

Show: weekend of 6 June, as the desk.

### 7 June — Armenia, parliament
Parliamentary. The prime minister comes from the assembly.

Show: weekend of 6 June.

### 7 June — Jersey, parliament
A crown dependency. One sentence on the same show.

### 7 June — Kosovo, parliament
Parliamentary, and the calendar’s sources describe it as another election after a deadlock. The president of Kosovo is indirect, later in the year (6 October). The public is choosing a parliament, not the president.

Show: weekend of 6 June.

### 7 June — Peru, president, second round
The runoff of the 12–13 April presidential vote. Congress is not elected again.

Show: weekend of 6 June.

### 14 June — Switzerland, referendums
Issues, not the Federal Council. Same lesson as March.

Show: weekend of 13 June.

### 21 June — Colombia, president, second round
The runoff. Congress stays as it was elected in March.

Show: weekend of 20 June.

### 28 June — New Caledonia, parliament
A territorial congress election, called after a constitutional plan failed. The show says “territorial parliament” and does not turn it into a French presidential vote. France’s own presidential election is April 2027.

Show: weekend of 27 June.

## July

### 2 July — Algeria, People’s National Assembly
The lower house. The president is not this ballot.

Show: weekend of 4 July.

### 4 July — Slovakia, referendum
A yes-or-no. Slovakia’s parliamentary election is undated on the 2027 calendar. This referendum does not elect that parliament.

Show: weekend of 4 July.

### 19 July — São Tomé and Príncipe, president
Presidential. The parliament of the same country is 27 September. Two offices, two dates.

Show: weekend of 18 July.

## August

### 12 August — Cook Islands, parliament
A territory in free association. Parliamentary. One block, not a two-hour show.

Show: weekend of 15 August.

### 13 August — Zambia, president and parliament
A general election: both offices, one period. The lesson against Cape Verde, which split them across May and November.

Show: weekend of 15 August. Preview on 8 August.

### 23 August — Kazakhstan, Kurultai
An election to the Kurultai, not a presidential election and not the March referendum. The host says the name of the body and does not call it “the Kazakh parliament” unless the week’s law card uses that name.

Show: weekend of 22 August.

### 29 August — Iceland, referendum
A yes-or-no on whether to continue European Union accession talks. It does not elect the Althing or the president.

Show: weekend of 29 August.

### 30 August — Guinea-Bissau, constitutional referendum
A yes-or-no. The president and the People’s National Assembly are a separate date, 6 December, and that December date is tentative on this desk.

Show: weekend of 29 August.

## September

### 13 September — Sweden, parliament
The Riksdag, 349 seats, four-year term. A party needs 4 percent nationally, or 12 percent in one constituency, to take seats. The prime minister is confirmed by the Riksdag under negative parliamentarism: the nominee takes office unless a majority votes no. The public does not elect the prime minister by name.

Show: weekend of 12 September. Preview on 5 September.

### 18 September — South Ossetia, president
A presidential vote in a territory whose status is disputed. The show says “presidential election, South Ossetia” and does not decide the status question. One block inside the Russia/Morocco week.

Show: weekend of 19 September.

### 18–20 September — Russia, State Duma
Three days for the lower house. The president of Russia is not on this ballot. The show lists the dates and the office. It does not issue a grade.

Show: weekend of 19 September.

### 23 September — Morocco, House of Representatives
The chamber the government is responsible to. Not a presidential election.

Show: weekend of 19 September as watch-next, and the weekend of 26 September as the desk.

### 24 September — Isle of Man, House of Keys
A crown dependency. One sentence on the 26 September show.

### 27 September — São Tomé and Príncipe, parliament
The legislature, two months after the president. Completes the pair that began on 19 July.

Show: weekend of 26 September.

### 27 September — Switzerland, referendums
Third referendum Sunday of the year. Same lesson, said shorter because the audience has heard it.

Show: weekend of 26 September.

## October

### 3 October — Latvia, Saeima
100 seats. Parliamentary. The president of Latvia is not on this ballot; the Saeima elects the president. Four-year term for the parliament.

Show: weekend of 3 October. Confirmed. This is the start of the two-hour Sunday.

### 4 October — Bosnia and Herzegovina, presidency and House of Representatives
Voters do not elect one head of state. One Bosniak and one Croat are elected in the Federation, and one Serb is elected in Republika Srpska. The House of Representatives, 42 seats, is elected the same day for four years.

Show: weekend of 3 October.

### 4 October — Brazil, president, Chamber of Deputies, and Senate
- President: majority of valid votes, or a runoff on 25 October. That runoff is not its own row until it is required. Governors use the same rule and are mentioned, not given their own national row.
- Chamber: all 513 seats, four-year terms.
- Senate: 54 of 81 seats, two in each state and the Federal District, eight-year terms, no runoff.

Show: weekend of 3 October.

### 9–10 October — Czech Republic, Senate, first round
One third of the 81 seats. Six-year terms. The Chamber of Deputies, which the government answers to, is not on this ballot. A second round, if a district needs one, falls on 16–17 October and is not listed as its own election until it is required.

Show: weekend of 10 October.

### 25 October — Bulgaria, president
Direct election of the president. Parliament was April. The show checks the week’s rule card for whether a majority is required and whether a runoff date has been set. It does not invent one.

Show: weekend of 24 October. This date is on the national calendar and is not yet a row in the website's `public/data/elections.json`.

### 25 October — Serbia, parliament
A snap election of the National Assembly. The president of Serbia called it for this day; the ordinary end of the term would have been later. Parliamentary, not presidential. The president is not on this ballot.

Show: weekend of 24 October. Not yet a row in the website's `public/data/elections.json`.

### 25 October — Brazil, presidential runoff, conditional
Only if no candidate won a majority on 4 October. The Chamber and the Senate are not voted again.

Show: weekend of 24 October, Open A or Open B.

### 27 October — Israel, Knesset
All 120 seats. A government needs a majority of the Knesset, 61. The prime minister is not directly elected. Pure proportional representation; a party needs 3.25 percent. The 25th Knesset was dissolved on 17 July 2026 after serving a full term, the first to do so since 1988.

Show: weekend of 31 October as the desk, previewed inside the 24 October show.

## November

### 3 November — United States, House, Senate, and governors
See [united-states.md](united-states.md). The presidency is not on the ballot.

Show: preview weekend of 31 October, desk weekend of 7 November.

### 7 November — New Zealand, House of Representatives
Parliamentary, three-year term. The governor-general asks a party leader who can command the house to form a government. The public does not elect a president.

Show: weekend of 7 November.

### 15 November — Cape Verde, president
The pair to the 17 May parliamentary election. Direct presidential vote. The assembly is not renewed again.

Show: weekend of 14 November.

### 28 November — Palestine, parliament
A legislative election. The show says “parliament” and the date. It does not invent a presidential election on the same day; the calendar line is the parliament.

Show: weekend of 28 November.

### 29 November — Switzerland, referendums
Fourth referendum Sunday. One sentence, unless a question on the ballot changes how the Federal Assembly works. Even then, the Federal Council is still elected by parliament.

Show: weekend of 28 November.

## December

### 5 December — The Gambia, president
Direct presidential election. Five-year term. The National Assembly is not this day. It is 10 April 2027.

Show: weekend of 5 December.

### 6 December — Guinea-Bissau, president and People’s National Assembly
On the international calendar as this day. On this desk’s website file the same day is **tentative**. The host says tentative. The August referendum was a different vote.

Show: weekend of 5 December.

### 13 December — Haiti, president, Chamber of Deputies, and Senate
The day published on international election calendars. Confirmed as a published day, which is not the same thing as a prediction that the vote will be calm or complete. The show says the offices and the published day.

Show: weekend of 12 December.

### 13 December — Transnistria, president
A presidential vote in a territory whose status is disputed. One sentence beside Haiti. The show does not decide the status.

Show: weekend of 12 December.

### 22 December — South Sudan, president, Transitional National Legislative Assembly, and Council of States
**Tentative.** Earlier announced dates were postponed. The line is not a promise that the vote will be held. The constitution of the transitional period is the structure lesson: these bodies are transitional offices, not an ordinary full term, until a source says the transition has ended.

Show: weekend of 19 December.

### Undated — Fiji, parliament
Due in 2026 on the calendar, with no day in this book. The 26 December show says “no day has been set” and does not pick a Saturday.

## How the year teaches, if you only remember six Sundays

| Sunday | Lesson |
| --- | --- |
| 8 February | A parliament, a presidential runoff, and a referendum can share a day and still be three different acts. |
| 8 March | Colombia changes congress months before it changes the president. |
| 12 April | Benin, Hungary, and Peru show three ways an executive is chosen. |
| 13 September | Sweden: the public elects a parliament, and the parliament tolerates a prime minister. |
| 4 October | Latvia’s parliament, Bosnia’s three-person presidency, Brazil’s majority rule. |
| 8 November | A United States midterm is not a presidential election. New Zealand’s parliament is on a three-year clock. |
